Zoom in: the nephron

The nephron does three things, in order. Watch a drop of blood become urine:

1FiltrationGlomerulus + Bowman's capsule›

Blood arrives under pressure at a tuft of leaky capillaries — the glomerulus. Water and small solutes are pushed out into the capsule, forming filtrate. Blood cells and proteins are too big to pass, so they stay behind.

2ReabsorptionProximal tubule · loop of Henle · distal tubule›

Most of the filtrate is worth keeping. As it travels the tubule, the body reclaims water, glucose, salts and bicarbonate back into the blood — fine-tuning exactly how much to keep.

3Secretion & concentrationDistal tubule · collecting duct›

Extra waste and surplus ions (like potassium and hydrogen) are actively added into the tubule. Water is drawn back out in the collecting duct, concentrating what remains into urine, which drains to the pelvis and ureter.

02 · Function

What healthy kidneys do

Filtering waste is only part of it. The kidneys are a control centre for the whole body's chemistry. A handy way to remember the jobs: A WET BED.

AAcid–base balanceKeeps the blood's pH steady›

Kidneys excrete acid and reclaim bicarbonate, holding blood pH in a narrow safe range. When they fail, acid builds up — metabolic acidosis.

WWater balanceSets how much fluid the body holds›

By adjusting how much water is reabsorbed, kidneys decide urine volume and keep total body fluid in check. Failure leads to fluid overload and oedema.

EErythropoietinSignals the marrow to make red cells›

Kidneys release the hormone EPO, which tells bone marrow to produce red blood cells. Less EPO in kidney failure means anaemia.

TToxin & waste removalClears urea, creatinine, drugs›

Nitrogen waste from protein breakdown and many drugs leave the body via the kidneys. When clearance drops, these accumulate — the state we call uraemia.

BBlood pressure controlThe renin–angiotensin system›

Kidneys sense pressure and release renin, triggering a cascade that manages salt, water and vessel tone. Kidney disease and high blood pressure feed each other.

EElectrolyte balanceSodium, potassium, calcium, phosphate›

Fine control of key ions keeps nerves and the heart working. Failure commonly causes a rise in potassium and phosphate — both clinically dangerous.

DVitamin D activationThe final step for strong bones›

Kidneys convert vitamin D into its active form so the gut can absorb calcium. Without it, bones and the calcium–phosphate balance suffer — renal bone disease.

Teaching point: notice how each failed function on this list maps to a treatment later — EPO loss → anaemia drugs, phosphate rise → binders, acid → buffered dialysate. The kidney's job list is the treatment list.

04 · Principles

How dialysis actually works

Strip it back and dialysis is just physics across a semipermeable membrane. Pick a principle and watch it happen — blood on the left, dialysate on the right.

Blood Dialysate
Urea & small waste Potassium Cells & protein (stay)

The three forces, together

›DiffusionClears solutes›

Waste moves from where it's crowded (blood) to where it's sparse (dialysate), down its concentration gradient. This is how urea, creatinine and potassium are cleared. Fresh dialysate keeps the gradient steep.

›UltrafiltrationRemoves water›

A pressure difference across the membrane pushes excess water out of the blood. This is how the machine takes off a patient's fluid gain between sessions — set as the UF goal.

›Countercurrent flowKeeps it efficient›

Blood and dialysate flow in opposite directions along the fibres. This keeps a concentration gradient the whole length of the dialyser, so clearance never stalls. Running them the same way would let them reach equilibrium and stop.

06 · The machine

Inside the dialysis machine

The machine does two jobs at once: it moves blood safely round the circuit, and it makes fresh dialysate to an exact recipe, all session long. Every part is either doing one of those jobs or watching for danger.

The machine

Pick a part

Blood side on the front, dialysate side behind the panels.

The dialysate side, in order

Behind the panels, treated water becomes dialysate in a fixed sequence. The machine checks it before it reaches the dialyser, and again on the way out.

1Treated water inFrom the RO loop›

Purified water arrives from the water treatment plant's distribution loop. The next chapter follows its journey from the tap.

2WarmedClose to body temperature›

A heater brings the water to the prescribed temperature.

Why it matters: dialysate set slightly below body temperature can help patients prone to low blood pressure, because it makes blood vessels tighten a little.
3De-airedDissolved gas drawn out›

Water holds dissolved air that would otherwise come out as bubbles in the dialyser, blocking fibres and confusing the sensors. The machine draws it out under low pressure first.

4MixedWater, acid and bicarbonate›

Pumps proportion the two concentrates into the water in exact ratios, making the prescribed recipe of sodium, potassium, calcium and bicarbonate.

5CheckedConductivity and temperature›

Sensors confirm the mix conducts electricity exactly as the right recipe should, and that it's at the right temperature.

If either is out: the machine goes into bypass. The fluid goes to drain and never reaches the dialyser.
6BalancedFresh in equals used out›

Balancing chambers make sure exactly as much used fluid leaves the dialyser as fresh fluid enters. A separate ultrafiltration pump then takes off the extra, the patient's UF goal, so fluid removal is precise.

7FilteredUltrapure before the dialyser›

Many machines pass the fresh dialysate through a final ultrafilter (on Fresenius machines, the DIASAFE) to remove remaining bacteria and endotoxin. It's what makes haemodiafiltration (HDF) possible, where fluid goes directly into the blood.

8Through the dialyserAgainst the blood flow›

Dialysate flows the opposite way to the blood, around the fibres, collecting waste and excess fluid as it goes.

9Blood leak checkLooking for red cells›

On the way out, an optical sensor watches the used dialysate for red cells, the sign of a torn membrane.

10To drainUsed once, then gone›

Spent dialysate goes to drain. It's never recirculated.

Why two concentrates?

The acid concentrate carries the sodium, potassium, calcium and magnesium, with a little acid to keep it stable. The bicarbonate is the buffer, usually from a dry cartridge (the bibag on Fresenius machines).

Mixed in advance, the calcium and magnesium would react with the bicarbonate and drop out as chalky deposits, blocking the machine and leaving the recipe wrong. So the machine proportions both into the water at the last moment, then checks the result.

Before and between patients

✓Self-test before every treatmentSensors, alarms and pressure holding›

Before each treatment the machine tests itself: its sensors, its alarms and whether the dialysate circuit holds pressure. A machine that fails its test isn't used until it has been checked and fixed.

↻DisinfectionBetween patients and on a schedule›

Between patients the machine runs a disinfection cycle, using heat, chemicals or both, on a schedule set by the manufacturer and the unit, alongside regular descaling. The dialysate side has to be clean before the next patient connects.

⚡Power cutBattery first, then by hand›

Most machines keep the blood pump running on battery for a while. If power can't be restored, the blood pump can be turned by hand to return the patient's blood, which is worth practising before you ever need it.

Kt/VLive clearance on the screenA check, not the measure›

Many machines, including the Fresenius models, can estimate clearance during the session from changes in dialysate conductivity, known as online clearance monitoring. It's a useful live check, but the QA bloods are what the unit's urea kinetic modelling is based on.

Machines differ. Alarm limits, responses and screens vary between models and software versions. Follow the prompts on your machine, the manufacturer's instructions and your unit's training.
07 · Water

Water fit for dialysis

Dialysate is almost all water, and a patient meets about 120 litres of it every session. Only a thin membrane stands between that water and the blood, so it has to be far purer than anything from a tap.

Why it has to be so pure

Water most people drink in a weekabout 14 L
Dialysate in a week of haemodialysisabout 360 L

When you drink water, your gut and kidneys stand between it and your blood. On dialysis only the membrane does, and anything small enough slips straight across. At a typical 500 mL/min for four hours, that's about 120 litres a session and 360 a week: more than most people drink in six months.

From tap to machine

Each stage removes something the next stage, or the patient, can't cope with. Switch one off and watch what gets through.

Sediment Calcium and magnesium Chlorine and chloramine Salts and metals Bacteria and endotoxin

Stage by stage

1Mains waterSafe to drink, not to dialyse›

Tap water carries chlorine or chloramine (added to kill germs), calcium and magnesium (hardness), traces of metals such as aluminium and copper, plus nitrates, fluoride, bacteria and endotoxin.

Fine for the gut and kidneys to handle. Not fine pushed across a membrane straight into the blood.
2Sediment filterParticles out first›

A multimedia filter traps sand, rust and silt so they don't clog the stages after it. It's backwashed regularly to clear what it has caught.

3Water softenerCalcium and magnesium swapped for sodium›

Resin beads exchange the hardness minerals for sodium. This mainly protects the RO membrane from scaling. The resin is regenerated with salt brine, so the brine tank needs keeping topped up.

4Carbon tanksChlorine and chloramine removed›

Activated carbon pulls out chlorine and chloramine, plus organic compounds. Two tanks run in series: the first does the work and the second is the backup. The sample point between them is where the chlorine check is done.

Why carbon comes before RO: RO can't be relied on to remove chloramine, and chlorine damages the RO membrane itself.
5Reverse osmosisThe heart of the system›

High pressure forces water through a membrane so fine it holds back almost all dissolved salts, metals, bacteria and endotoxin. Two streams leave: purified product water, and a reject stream that carries the concentrated contaminants to drain.

Watched continuously: product water conductivity. A rise means the membrane is letting more through.
6Distribution loopAlways moving, no dead ends›

Product water circulates constantly through a ring main to every machine and back. Stagnant water lets bacteria grow biofilm, a slimy layer that sheds endotoxin and is very hard to remove, so the loop has no dead ends and is disinfected on a schedule.

7At the machineOne last filter›

Many machines add a final ultrafilter on the fresh dialysate, catching remaining bacteria and endotoxin to make it ultrapure.

The chlorine check

◷Every treatment dayTotal chlorine, between the tanks›

Total chlorine, meaning free chlorine plus chloramine, is tested at the start of each treatment day or shift as the unit's water procedure sets out, from the sample point between the two carbon tanks. The result should be under 0.1 mg/L.

?Why between the tanks?An early warning›

If the first tank is exhausted, the test catches it while the second tank is still protecting patients. Testing only after the last tank would discover the problem with no protection left.

!A positive resultEscalate straight away›

Chlorine after the first tank means the backup is now the only protection. Tell the nurse in charge and the technical team at once, and follow the water procedure exactly: it sets out what to retest and when dialysis can continue.

Testing and limits

Bacteria in dialysis waterunder 100 CFU/mLAction at 50
Endotoxin in dialysis waterunder 0.25 EU/mLAction at 0.125
Ultrapure dialysis fluidunder 0.1 CFU/mLand endotoxin under 0.03 EU/mL

CFU counts living bacteria. EU measures endotoxin, the fragments bacteria leave behind, which still cause fevers. Both are sampled routinely, commonly monthly, from the loop and the machines, and a full chemical analysis, including aluminium, is done periodically. Action levels sit below the limits so a problem is caught early.

Lessons that wrote the rules

AlAluminiumDialysis dementia›

In the 1970s, aluminium in dialysis water caused dialysis encephalopathy, a progressive and often fatal dementia, alongside bone disease and anaemia. Reverse osmosis is the main reason it's now rare.

ClChloramineHaemolysis›

Chloramine crossing into the blood damages red cells until they burst, causing haemolytic anaemia. Outbreaks have followed carbon tanks becoming exhausted, and water suppliers changing their disinfection without the unit knowing.

CaCalcium and magnesiumHard water syndrome›

Hardness reaching the dialysate causes nausea, vomiting, headache, a burning feeling in the skin and high blood pressure during treatment, usually when a softener fails or isn't regenerated.

●Bacteria and endotoxinFevers and rigors›

Contamination causes fevers, rigors and low blood pressure during dialysis. Lower-level exposure over years adds to chronic inflammation, which is one reason ultrapure fluid matters.

!Algal toxinsBrazil, 1996›

At a dialysis unit in Caruaru, toxins from algae in an inadequately treated water supply caused acute liver failure, and dozens of patients died. It remains the starkest reminder of why every stage is checked.

Check your unit's water procedure. The limits here follow ISO 23500 and UK Kidney Association guidance. The renal technical team usually looks after the plant itself, and your unit's procedure sets the testing schedule and who does each check, but everyone on the floor should know what an abnormal result means.
08 · HDF

HD and haemodiafiltration

Haemodialysis cleans the blood mostly by diffusion. Haemodiafiltration, or HDF, adds convection: it pushes litres of plasma water through the membrane so that larger molecules are dragged out with it, then replaces that water with sterile fluid the machine makes as it goes. At ENHT it is the standard treatment for anyone whose blood flow is good enough.

Trust REN 050

Post or pre: which and when

PostPost-dilutionThe Trust standard›

The most efficient mode for clearing middle molecules, because blood reaches the membrane undiluted. The guideline recommends post-dilution generally. In the record it is HDF_Post.

The limit: filtration of about 25 to 30% of the blood flow. With HDF Auto Sub ticked, the 5008 and 6008 manage this themselves.
PrePre-dilutionIf the circuit clots, or anticoagulation isn’t possible›

The blood arrives thinner, so it filters easily and clots less. The guideline considers it when the circuit is clotting, or the patient can’t have adequate anticoagulation because of an allergy or bleeding risk. In the record it is HDF_Pre.

Not for low blood flow: below 300 mL/min, HDF is suspended instead. And pre-dilution needs roughly 1.5 to 2 times the volume for the same clearance.
MidMid-dilutionNamed in the guideline›

The fluid joins partway along a specially designed dialyser. Like pre-dilution, it is less efficient than post-dilution, which is why the guideline recommends post-dilution generally.

HFHaemofiltrationConvection only›

No dialysate flows past the membrane, so all the clearance comes from convection. It is rarely used for regular outpatient treatment; continuous haemofiltration, CVVH, is used in intensive care.

    Getting the most from HDF

    The guideline’s four ways to optimise high-flow HDF.

    Maximise blood flow
    • Larger AVF needles, where appropriate.
    • Raise the blood flow in 50 mL/min steps to reach 350 or more.

    • Blood flow.

    The evidence

    The Trust guideline gives three reasons for HDF. It removes β2-microglobulin more efficiently than HD, which can delay the disabling joint disease it causes, dialysis amyloidosis. It lowers advanced glycation end-products, which can cause cardiovascular disease, more than high-flux HD. And it may improve survival.REN 050

    Patients with very little residual kidney function are likely to benefit most, because working kidneys also clear β2-microglobulin. Other benefits, such as better phosphate control or less itching, have been reported but also disputed, and HDF isn’t a treatment for low blood pressure during dialysis.REN 050

    The guideline cites ESHOL, a Spanish trial from 2013 that found lower mortality with high-efficiency post-dilution online HDF, and a long-term analysis by Vilar and colleagues in 2009: across 858 patients over 18 years, those on HDF alone had about a third lower risk of death than those on high-flux HD.

    Since then, the CONVINCE trial in 2023 randomised 1,360 adults to high-dose HDF, aiming for at least 23 litres of convection per session, or to high-flux HD. Over a median of 30 months, 17.3% of the HDF group died against 21.9% of the HD group. Its 23 litres counts the fluid removed as well as the substitution fluid, so it sits close to the Trust’s over 20 litres of substitution.

    Maduell and colleagues, Journal of the American Society of Nephrology, 2013. Vilar and colleagues, Clinical Journal of the American Society of Nephrology, 2009. Blankestijn and colleagues, New England Journal of Medicine, 2023.

    09 · Access

    Getting to the blood

    Haemodialysis needs a reliable, high-flow route into the circulation. There's a clear order of preference — the phrase is fistula first.

    ◆ Most preferred
    1Arteriovenous fistula (AVF)Artery joined to a vein›

    A surgeon connects an artery to a nearby vein, usually in the arm. Over weeks the vein toughens and enlarges under arterial flow — it "matures" — giving a durable vessel to cannulate.

    Why first: lowest infection and clotting risk, longest lasting. The trade-off is it must be planned ahead and needs time to mature before use.
    2Arteriovenous graft (AVG)Synthetic tube bridges the two›

    When veins aren't suitable for a fistula, a soft synthetic tube links artery to vein. It can be used sooner than a fistula.

    Trade-off: more prone to clotting and infection than a fistula, but a strong option when native vessels won't do.
    3Central venous catheter (CVC)Line into a large central vein›

    A tube sits in a large vein (often in the neck). It works immediately, so it's used when access is needed urgently or while a fistula matures.

    Why last: highest risk of bloodstream infection and can damage the vein over time. Best kept temporary where possible.

    Watch the access for trouble

    Infection — redness, discharge or fever, especially with lines. The leading serious risk.
    Thrombosis / stenosis — narrowing or clot. A weak or absent thrill, or poor flows, are warning signs.
    Aneurysm — ballooning from repeated needling in one spot. Rotate cannulation sites.
    Steal syndrome — the access diverts too much blood, leaving the hand cold or painful.

    A quick "look, listen, feel" each session — inspect the site, listen for a bruit, feel for a thrill — catches most problems early.

    Trust REN 050

    Access and HDF

    HDF depends on the access: the Trust looks for a blood flow of 300 mL/min or more, consistently and without frequent alarms, on any access type.

    Surveillance. Access flow is checked regularly, looking for an AVF flow above 600 mL/min, and poor flows or recirculation are escalated. Measuring with Transonic or BTM follows REN 057.
    Needles. Larger AVF needles, where appropriate, help reach the blood flow HDF needs.
    Below 300 mL/min. Nurses suspend HDF in the dialysis orders until the access problem is resolved. Clinical support workers tell the nurse in charge.
    10 · Complications

    What can go wrong

    Two timescales to know: things that happen during a session, and the longer-term effects of living with kidney failure.

    ↓HypotensionThe most common intradialytic event›

    Removing fluid too fast, or more than the patient can refill from the tissues, drops the blood pressure — causing dizziness, nausea or cramps. Managed by adjusting fluid removal and the target weight.

    ✱Muscle crampsOften legs, late in the session›

    Linked to rapid fluid and electrolyte shifts. Common alongside hypotension.

    ◐Nausea, headache & fatigueThe "washed-out" feeling›

    Frequently follow blood-pressure and fluid changes during and just after treatment.

    ≈Disequilibrium syndromeClearing waste too fast›

    If urea is removed very quickly — usually early in a patient's dialysis life — fluid shifts into the brain, causing headache and confusion. Prevented by starting gently.

    !Access & circuit eventsBleeding, clotting, reactions›

    Needle dislodgement, clotting of the circuit, or reactions to the membrane. Why the circuit is monitored closely throughout — pressures, air detection and alarms.

    ◔AnaemiaToo few red cells›

    Falling EPO and iron stores leave patients tired and breathless. Managed with EPO-type medicines and iron.

    ⬡Bone–mineral disorderCalcium, phosphate & vitamin D›

    Phosphate builds up and active vitamin D falls, disturbing calcium and weakening bone (renal bone disease). Managed with diet, binders and vitamin D.

    ♥Cardiovascular diseaseThe leading cause of death›

    Fluid load, high blood pressure and mineral changes strain the heart and vessels over time.

    ◇Fluid & diet limitsLiving between sessions›

    Patients often limit fluid, potassium, phosphate and salt to stay safe between treatments — a big part of daily life on dialysis.

    ⌘Infection riskEspecially with lines›

    Repeated access to the bloodstream and an altered immune response raise infection risk — a major reason to protect and prefer fistulas.

    11 · Medicines

    The medicine, by the problem it solves

    Almost every renal medicine replaces a job the kidney can no longer do. Read each one as: kidney fails → this problem → this group fixes it.

    ◔Anaemia agentsEPO-type medicines + iron›

    Replace the red-cell signal and its raw materials, easing tiredness and breathlessness.

    Why: failing kidneys make less erythropoietin, so the marrow makes fewer red cells. Iron is given alongside because the marrow needs it to respond.
    ⬢Phosphate bindersTaken with food›

    Bind phosphate in the gut so less is absorbed from meals.

    Why: kidneys normally clear phosphate. When they can't, it rises and pulls calcium from bone — driving renal bone disease. Binders must be taken with meals to work.
    ☀Active vitamin DThe form the body can use›

    Supplies vitamin D already in its active form.

    Why: the kidney does the final activation step. Without it, calcium isn't absorbed and bones suffer — so the active form is given directly.
    ⚡Potassium-lowering agentsBinders taken by mouth›

    Bind potassium in the gut to bring dangerously high levels down.

    Why: kidneys normally excrete potassium. When it accumulates it threatens the heart's rhythm, so binders help control it between sessions.
    ↓Blood-pressure medicinesPlus fluid removal on dialysis›

    Lower blood pressure and, together with ultrafiltration, manage fluid load.

    Why: kidney disease and high blood pressure worsen each other. Much of a patient's fluid control also comes from the UF goal at each session.
    ≋Buffer for acidosisDelivered in the dialysate›

    Bicarbonate in the dialysate neutralises the acid that builds up.

    Why: the kidney's acid-clearing job is taken over by the treatment itself — the dialysate is buffered to correct blood pH.
    ✚AnticoagulationDuring the session only›

    Keeps blood from clotting as it moves through the tubing and dialyser.

    Why: blood clots on contact with the artificial surfaces of the circuit. A short-acting anticoagulant during treatment keeps it flowing.
    ◈Water-soluble vitaminsReplacing what's washed out›

    Top up vitamins lost across the membrane during each session.

    Why: small water-soluble vitamins (B and C groups) diffuse into the dialysate along with the waste, so they're replaced.
    Remember: this app names groups and reasons, not doses or specific drugs. Always check the individual patient's plan and your local formulary.
    12 · Monitoring

    QA bloods & urine

    Quality Assurance is how we prove the dialysis is doing enough, and how each patient's dose gets individualised. The numbers only mean something if the samples were taken exactly right — so here, technique is the test.

    Trust REN 034

    What it's all for

    The delivered dose of dialysis is directly linked to how patients do, so the department uses urea kinetic modelling to check what each patient is actually receiving and to set an individualised prescription. Two measurements feed it: how much the dialyser cleared (the QA pre/post pair) and how much the patient's own kidneys still clear (KRU, from the urine collection). Results are reviewed at the weekly QA meetings, and a Kt/V that is under- or over-achieving gets referred to the senior nurse or doctor. The QA meeting is also where the consultant checks each patient’s blood flow and treatment mode against the HDF criteria.REN 050

    Teaching line: we aren't collecting numbers for a file. A wrong sample can change how long someone sits in that chair every week.
    Trust Doc 2077

    Taking the samples

    Two samples around one session, each with its own procedure. Tap between them.

    1Before dialysis startsAfter needling or removing the line lock›

    The sample is taken as part of putting the patient on, once the needle is in or the line limb lock is off, and before haemodialysis begins.

    Non-negotiable: dilution with saline or anticoagulant must be avoided. If dialysis has already started, or the sample is diluted, the result is inaccurate and the sample should be discarded — not sent and caveated.
    2Fistula only — discard 10 mLThe step most often missed›

    With a fistula or graft, withdraw 10 mL of blood and discard it first. Clamp the needle, remove the syringe and place it carefully at the edge of the sterile pack.

    Why: it clears diluted blood from the needle so what you sample is the patient's actual circulating blood.
    3Draw the sampleConnector to needle or RDC limb›

    Connect the blood sampling connector and tube to the fistula needle or RDC limb, release the needle clamp (fistula only) and withdraw what's needed. Dispose of the connector into the sharps bin, then initiate haemodialysis as prescribed so the access doesn't clot.

    4Label at the patient's sideNever away from the chair›

    Label the tubes while still with the patient, so the sample can't be misattributed.

    5Centrifuge if potassium or U&EOther pre bloods don't need it›

    A serum potassium or U&E must be centrifuged for an accurate serum result. Other pre-dialysis bloods don't require it. Send everything with the ICE request form, then document in the patient's electronic record.

    1PPE and swab the port30 seconds, and let it dry›

    Hand hygiene, then gloves, apron and eye protection — this step risks blood splashes. Swab the arterial sampling port with an alcohol or chlorhexidine 2% wipe for a full 30 seconds.

    2Drop to 100 mL/min for 30 secondsThe critical step›

    At the completion of dialysis, decrease the blood flow to 100 mL/min for 30 seconds.

    Why: it fills the arterial blood line with non-recirculated blood, so you sample the patient rather than blood that has just crossed the dialyser. Skip it and the urea reads falsely low.
    3Draw with the pump still runningFrom the arterial sampling port›

    Leave the blood pump running and withdraw the sample from the arterial sampling port using the appropriate connector, needle and tube. Dispose of sharps, then continue with disconnection as normal.

    4Label, centrifuge, sendAnd document›

    Label at the patient's side, centrifuge the sample, and send to pathology with the ICE request form — by courier if needed. Record in the patient's electronic notes that the bloods have gone.

    ◔Mid-dialysis samplesMainly blood sugar›

    A mid-dialysis sample may be needed for blood glucose monitoring, taken the same way from the swabbed arterial port but without the flow reduction — then continue with point-of-care testing.

    The three-minute rule: if dialysis has been interrupted for more than three minutes for any reason, the samples are inaccurate and must not be used for urea kinetic modelling.

    Why the flow reduction matters

    Those 30 seconds at 100 mL/min are the difference between measuring the patient and measuring the machine. Three things work against you:

    ↺Access recirculationClean blood re-entering the arterial needle›

    Blood returning through the venous needle can be drawn straight back into the arterial needle — especially if the needles sit close together, the lines are reversed, or access flow is poor. That blood has already crossed the dialyser, so its urea is low.

    ♥Cardiopulmonary recirculationClean blood round the heart and lungs›

    Cleaned blood returning to the heart and lungs can come back round to the access before it has mixed with the urea still sitting in the body's tissues. Again — a falsely low reading.

    ↗Urea reboundWhy the number climbs after you stop›

    Once the pump stops, urea moves out of the tissues back into the blood, so the level rises again over the next half hour or so. This is why the sample is taken at the completion of dialysis rather than later, and why an equilibrated Kt/V reads lower than the simple one.

    What's at stake: a falsely low post urea makes the URR and Kt/V look better — so an under-dialysed patient appears adequate and nobody acts. The 30 seconds is a patient-safety step, not a formality.

    The adequacy sum

    The pre and post pair answer one question: did this session clear enough? The laboratory does the calculation — but knowing how it moves tells you when a result looks wrong. Drag the sliders.

    70 % urea reduction
    Adequate

    %URR — urea reduction ratioThe simple one›

    The percentage fall in urea across one session: pre minus post, divided by pre. Quick to picture and easy to explain, but it ignores the fluid removed and the patient's size.

    Commonly used minimum: at least 65%.
    Kt/VKt/VWhat the prescription is set on›

    Goes further than URR by also accounting for the fluid removed and the patient's total body water, so it compares fairly between a large patient and a small one. This is the figure used for urea kinetic modelling and the one reviewed at QA meetings.

    The figures: around 1.2 is the usual minimum for three sessions a week, and results here typically sit in the 1.4 range. Both under-achieving and over-achieving Kt/V get referred to the senior nurse or doctor.
    ⇄Cross-check the pairWhen a result doesn't add up›

    URR and Kt/V come from the same two bottles, so they move together — a Kt/V around 1.4 sits roughly alongside a URR near 70%. If one month they disagree sharply, or a patient's usually steady figures swing without their prescription changing, suspect the sampling before you suspect the dialysis.

    Trust ICE sets

    The two QA sets

    The round is ordered as two request sets across two sessions — the names below match the screen you'll order from.

    1Urea, Electrolytes and Creat (U&E)Pre-dialysis, at connection›

    The pre sample for the adequacy pair, taken before the anticoagulant and before any flush. Centrifuge it, since it's a U&E.

    2Post Dialysis urea & CreatinineEnd of the same session›

    Drawn after 30 seconds at 100 mL/min. It does two jobs: it pairs with the pre U&E for URR and Kt/V, and it establishes the blood urea at the start of the KRU collection.

    3Urine Interdialytic (IDUR)Containers issued›

    Give the patient the right number of two-litre containers for their usual output, labelled with name and hospital number, plus the patient information sheet. The ICE request form is printed today and kept in their dialysis equipment box, ready for the next session.

    ◔Full Blood Count (FBC)Haemoglobin›

    Sets the anaemia treatment — whether the EPO-type medicine goes up, down or holds. The trend matters more than any single value.

    ≋BicarbonateOrdered separately here›

    Shows whether the acid load is being corrected between sessions. A persistently low result can prompt a change to the dialysate buffer.

    ⚡Urea, Electrolytes and Creat (U&E)Pre-dialysis›

    Potassium, sodium, urea and creatinine. This one also closes the KRU window — but only take it once you've confirmed the urine collection is complete and over 150 mL.

    ⬡Bone ProfileCalcium, phosphate, alkaline phosphatase›

    Drives binder and vitamin D decisions. A rising phosphate is often a conversation about diet and about whether binders are being taken with meals, rather than a straight dose increase.

    FeTransferrin SaturationIron actually available›

    How much iron is available to the marrow right now, as opposed to how much is in store. Read together with ferritin and the haemoglobin.

    CRPC Reactive Protein (CRP)Inflammation›

    Flags infection or inflammation — which both blunts the response to anaemia treatment and pushes ferritin up. A high CRP changes how you read the iron results, so it isn't an optional extra.

    ◈FerritinIron stores›

    The stored iron figure. There's no point pushing an EPO-type medicine into a patient with nothing to build red cells from — but ferritin rises with inflammation, so it's read next to the CRP.

    The extra tiers

    Alongside the monthly QA sets, three further sets run on longer cycles.

    3m3 monthly extra HD testsSeven items›

    Liver function test, glucose, HbA1c monitoring, parathyroid hormone, the RDU 3-monthly dialysis screen, a Staph aureus screen, and a haemodialysis catheter exit site culture.

    Note the pattern: PTH sits here rather than monthly because it's read as a slower trend alongside the bone profile. The screens and exit-site culture are infection surveillance — they protect the whole unit, not just that patient.
    6m6 monthly extra HD testsTwo items›

    Glucose, and beta-2 microglobulin.

    Why beta-2: it's a middle molecule that healthy kidneys clear but dialysis removes far less efficiently. Over years it can accumulate and deposit in joints, so it's tracked over the long term. It’s the main middle molecule HDF is used to clear: see chapter 8.
    1yAnnual extra HD testsOne item›

    Hepatitis B immunity, post-vaccination.

    Why annually: vaccine protection wanes, and immunity is often weaker in kidney failure. Checking the antibody level shows whether a patient is still protected or needs a booster — important where patients share a unit.
    Trust REN 029

    The urine collection (KRU)

    KRU is residual renal urea clearance — how much the patient's own kidneys still clear. It contributes to their total clearance, so it directly shapes the prescription. It declines rapidly once dialysis starts, which is why every patient who passes urine has it measured.

    1Start day — empty firstBefore dialysis begins›

    The patient must completely empty their bladder, urostomy bag or catheter bag before the session starts. That urine is not collected — it starts the window from empty.

    Then, on completion of that session: take the post-dialysis urea and creatinine. That establishes the blood urea at the start of the KRU window.
    2Issue the containersTwo-litre, named and numbered›

    Give the right number of two-litre containers for that patient's usual interdialytic output, labelled with their name and hospital number, along with the patient information sheet. If they pass more than two litres in two days, they need an extra bottle. Instruct them to collect all urine up to their next appointment.

    3Which sessions?Different for twice-weekly patients›

    On three-times-weekly dialysis the collection starts on the first dialysis of the week and finishes on the second.

    On twice-weekly dialysis it starts on the second dialysis of the week and finishes on the first dialysis of the following week.

    4End day — ask before you takeThe order matters›

    First, check with the patient whether any of the collection was missed. Remember they empty their bladder before this session too, and that goes in.

    Only if the collection is complete and over 150 mL: take the pre-dialysis U&E, then send the blood and the urine together with the ICE forms. The urine goes for urea, protein, creatinine and volume.
    5Incomplete or under 150 mLSend nothing›

    If any was missed, or the volume is under 150 mL, do not send the urine or the blood. Discard the collection, cancel the test, and tell the dialysis nurse so it can be rescheduled.

    Why so strict: an incomplete collection understates residual function — and since KRU feeds the prescription, that can wrongly lengthen a patient's dialysis time. Saying "some was missed" is the right answer, every time.
    Trust REN 029

    The QA cycle

    Two sessions, two request sets, and the urine collected across the gap between them — but the gap differs by dialysis pattern:

    ◷Who's in and who isn'tScope of the programme›

    All chronic kidney disease patients on the haemodialysis programme, including those transferring in from other units or therapies. Holiday patients are excluded, as are patients who decline to follow the procedure.

    ✎ReschedulingWhose job it is›

    Re-scheduling incomplete tests sits with the registered nurses and primary nurses, and support workers are expected to report when a requested test hasn't been taken. Both halves of the pair need rebooking together — one set without the other gives neither adequacy nor KRU.

    Getting it right — the usual slips

    Post sample at full speed — skipping the 30 seconds at 100 mL/min. Flatters both URR and Kt/V.
    Forgetting the 10 mL discard — fistula and graft pre samples only. Without it you're measuring diluted blood.
    Sampling after dialysis has started — or after a flush. Discard it; don't send it caveated.
    Not centrifuging — potassium and U&E samples need it for an accurate serum result.
    Haemolysis — damaged cells leak potassium and give a falsely high result. Avoid excessive suction and rough handling.
    Sending a short collection — under 150 mL or incomplete goes nowhere. Cancel and reschedule.
    Interrupted session — over three minutes and the samples can't be used for modelling.
    Labelling away from the chair — label at the patient's side, every time.
